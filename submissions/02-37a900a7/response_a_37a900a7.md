What you’re describing is a **real conversational pattern**, and it has a name in communication research: one person is doing *co-construction* (adding meaning, building on ideas, validating) while the other is mostly doing *turn-taking* (sharing their own material without weaving yours in). It can feel invisible because both people are being “polite.”

## Likely reasons (not necessarily malicious)

**1. Different conversational cultures**
Some people grow up in families where the norm is: *talk about yourself, then the other person talks about themselves.* No one expects “and therefore…” or “that reminds me of…” They aren’t refusing to engage; they literally weren’t raised to.

**2. Roles have silently formed**
You may have become *The Processor* (the one who validates, deepens, makes meaning) and she has become *The Reporter* (the one who transmits events). Once that role split locks in, it feels unfair because you’re doing two jobs.

**3. You may be over-giving without realizing it**
If you reliably add 3 layers of insight/validity, she only needs to add 0. It’s not “fair,” but it’s predictable: **over-functioning invites under-functioning.**

**4. Active vs. passive listening**
She may listen politely (eye contact, “wow!”) but treat your sharing as *finished*, whereas you treat her sharing as *a draft to improve with her.* Those are different tasks.

**5. Confirmation bias check (important)**
Before concluding, track 3–5 exchanges over a week. Note: did she ever say “yes, and…” to your idea? Did you ever just say “wow, that sucks” to hers without adding? We often over-give to people we care about and under-notice when we do.

## What to do (lowest drama first)

### Step 1: Run a 2-week “under-functioning” test
Stop adding extra insight when she speaks. Only validate *lightly* (“That sounds frustrating”). 
Stop adding extra insight when *you* speak. Share the headline and stop. See what happens:
- Does she ask a follow-up? (Good—role may be flexible.)
- Does she just pivot to her next story? (Then it’s a style/pattern, not an accident.)

### Step 2: Meta-communicate with low stakes
Not during a bad moment. Use a *behavioral* reframe, not an accusation:

> “I’ve noticed our talks sometimes drift where I feel like I’m adding a lot of analysis and not getting much back, and when you share I flood it with extra ideas. Can we try staying with one topic a little longer and building on each other’s point before switching?”

Or simpler:
> “I’d love it if when I tell you something, you told me what stood out to you about it before we move on—and I’ll do the same for you.”

### Step 3: Specific in-the-moment scripts
- When she finishes a story: instead of your usual 2 insights, try: “What part of that is still bothering you?” (forces her to go deeper, not you).
- When you finish yours: “What do you think about that?” (explicit ask, no free insight).
- If she pivots instead of answering: “Hmm, I’m still curious what you thought of X—I’m not ready to leave it yet.”

## How to judge whether this is “unfair” or just “different”

Ask yourself:
- Does she show up for you in *other* currencies (rides, gifts, checking in when you’re sad, remembering small things)? If yes—this may be a **modality mismatch**, not inequity.
- Is this pattern *only* with you, or with everyone? (If everyone, it’s her style.)
- After you pull back your extra effort, does she *escalate curiosity* or *escalate broadcasting*? The latter suggests the friendship may be more extractive than you want.

## A hard truth
If you reduce your contribution and she doesn’t notice for weeks, that tells you something: she wasn’t receiving what you were giving—she was consuming it. That’s not “fair,” and you’re allowed to downgrade the friendship to “pleasant but low-depth” rather than expecting a peer in that specific way.

You don’t need her to be malicious for it to be annoying. But *not* fixing it after you name it would be a red flag.
