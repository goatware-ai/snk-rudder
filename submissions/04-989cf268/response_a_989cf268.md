**Cover story / day-one logistics (late 1991)**  
I arrive in October 1991 in suburban Philadelphia (Commodore’s US base is in West Chester, PA). I don’t show up with a suitcase of fusion reactors; I show up with about $2,000 in period cash, a forged 1991 resume that’s merely “good enough,” and a portfolio of *specific, stealable* prototypes. My goal is not to become CEO in a week. My goal is to get hired as a **multimedia developer-relations flack or junior product manager on the Amiga team** and then make myself useful to the two people who can actually change direction: the engineers in the Skunk Works chip group and whoever is currently whispering to Irving Gould (the money) and Mehdi Ali (the hatchet-man president).

---

### The “normal person” playbook (1991–1993)

**1. Stop the bleeding in the one place a junior hire can influence: third-party software.**  
Commodore’s fatal flaw wasn’t just bad hardware; it was a shrinking dev ecosystem while the PC clone market commoditized everything. I don’t control the budget, but I *can* build **one unignorable demo** in 1992:

- A real-time 3D viewport running in an Amiga window alongside a 2D paint program (pre-empting what Silicon Graphics and Autodesk will later charge $20 k for).  
- Show it to the team as: *“This is what Photoshop 3.0 + early 3ds max look like in 1994. If Agnus/Alice doesn’t get a unified memory controller and a 68040 option by Q4 1992, Adobe writes us off.”*

**2. Kill the CDTV/CD-i trap before it ships.**  
CDTV was already burning cash in 1991. I use internal newsletters and after-hours brown-bag sessions to push the argument: *“A sealed-box living-room consumer appliance with no expandability is a Sega/Nintendo fight we will lose. Re-badge the same box as a**$499 developer SKD + 68030 tower** and sell it to studios.”*  
A normal person can’t cancel CDTV, but a vocal, well-documented internal champion can give the engineering managers cover to re-allocate two designers from the CDTV team to the **AGA chipset + “Walker” (A6000) program** six months earlier.

**3. The “Trojan Horse” x86 bridge (1992–1993).**  
By mid-1992 even mid-level people at Commodore knew 68k was doomed; they were paralyzed. I don’t have the clout to pivot to PowerPC yet, but I *can* prototype a **AmigaOS 3.x hypervisor / DOS box + Mac emulator stack on a 80386SX board** (not full virtualization, but a fast enough 68 k emulator + native DOS window).  
I don’t bury it in research. I put it on a floppy labeled *“AmigaDOS 4.0 preview: runs Excel 4.0 *and* Deluxe Paint in one desktop—MS Office license deal pending.”*  
If even 15 % of Amigas in 1993 can legally run a DOS/Windows window, Commodore suddenly looks like an “upgrade path” to corporate buyers instead of a dead-end.

**4. Grassroots open licensing (the boring admin route).**  
I get myself assigned to **OEM outreach** (a job nobody else wants). I draft a 4-page one-sheet template that any PC clone maker can sign to build an “Amiga 5000” motherboard: Commodore gets $15/unit royalty, the OEM gets a differentiated machine, and the OS becomes standard. I mail 40 of these on Commodore letterhead to AST, Gateway, and Compaq’s advanced-systems group. 38 go in the bin; 2 start a conversation that becomes **the template Escom/Gateway later use when they buy the assets in 1995**—but maybe three years earlier.

**5. Don’t fight Mehdi Ali in the open—outflank him with metrics.**  
Ali famously slashed R&D and marketing to please banks. As a nobody, I lose a direct fight. Instead I start a **zero-budget “Amiga Developer Satisfaction Survey”** (paper forms, Usenet, local user groups). I get 200 developers to fill it out. I type the results into a three-page memo: *“87 % of respondents say they will ship next title on MS-DOS if no 68040 / 2 MB RAM option ships under $999 by Q3 1993.”*  
I CC the head of US sales *and* the external accountants. If the bankers see a documented attrition cliff, they sometimes force a R&D reprieve themselves.

### What I realistically expect (hedge)

Commodore had ~$100 M+ in debt, a revolving-door C-suite, and was bleeding ~$20 M/quarter by 1993. A normal person with future knowledge probably **does not “save” Commodore as an independent company**. The realistic win conditions are:

| Outcome | What a normal person can tilt toward |
|---|---|
| **Controlled merger (best case)** | Amiga OS + custom chips licensed to IBM or HP in 1992 instead of rotting in bankruptcy |
| **Asset sale 1994 (likely)** | Escom/Gateway buys IP for *more* and *sooner* because third-party interest was warmed up in 1992 |
| **Brand survival** | Commodore name stays on consumer audio or controllers—think what happens if someone files defensive trademarks early and parks them under a spin-off |

### The personal arc (to answer the “normal person” part)
- **Months 1–3:** Get the job, rent a studio in Exton, PA, learn LightWave and Deluxe Paint.  
- **Months 4–12:** Build the demos *after hours* because my official job is booth duty at CES.  
- **Year 2:** Become the “crazy demo guy” who hands a disk to journalists at AmigaWorld and InfoWorld retreats.  
- **Year 3:** Either I am ignored and watch the April 1994 liquidation from the parking lot, or a mid-level exec green-lights the OEM kit and the company ends 1995 as an **IP licensor** rather than a corpse.

Either way, I don’t “save” the 1980s Commodore. I try to make sure the corpse has a will.
