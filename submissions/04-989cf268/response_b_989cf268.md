If I land in, say, 1990 with nothing but a suitcase, a Social Security number I have to forge, and a head full of future knowledge, I need to be brutally honest with myself: **one normal person cannot stop Commodore International from imploding.**

The bankruptcy in April 1994 wasn’t caused by one bad product decision that a clever employee could fix. It was structural—Irving Gould running the company like a personal fiefdom from Canada, the Amiga division starved for R&D cash while being forced to push the CD32 (a console-with-no-games into a PC-dominated market), and a board that fundamentally misunderstood technology marketing. I’m not on that board. I don’t have $100 million. I can’t fire Mehdi Ali.

So as a *normal* time traveler, I don’t try to “save the corporation.” I try to **save the soul of the company** and use my knowledge to become the capital that actually acquires it after the fall.

Here is my realistic 1990s playbook:

**Phase 1: 1990–1993 — Make the capital I don’t have**
I don’t try to get a job at Commodore in West Chester, Pennsylvania hoping to “fix it from inside.” I’m a random person; I’d be in customer support or loading boxes, not the boardroom.

Instead, I live like a monk and invest every spare dollar in the tech boom I know is coming:
- **Microsoft** (1990 price split-adjusted: cheap, and I know Windows 3.1/95 is coming).
- **Intel** (the 486/Pentium wave is about to dominate).
- Maybe a little **Apple** in 1990–1991 (before the PowerPC pivot, but I know they survive and thrive).

I avoid Commodore stock like the plague—I *know* it goes to zero in ’94.

By 1993, I’m not Bill Gates, but I might have turned $20,000 into a few hundred thousand, or more if I’m disciplined.

**Phase 2: 1993–1994 — Watch the ship sink, but map the wreck**
I watch the CD32 flop in Europe. I watch the Amiga 4000T get canceled. I watch Commodore bleed cash. I start networking with Amiga engineers, user groups, and liquidators. I study exactly which patents, trademarks, and engineering teams are worth saving (the Amiga chipset, the OS, the remaining staff like Dave Haynie).

**Phase 3: April 1994 — Buy the corpse**
Commodore files for liquidation. The assets are sold off in pieces. In real history, Escom bought the Amiga tech for about $14 million, then went bankrupt itself, and the brand ended up in the hands of Gateway 2000.

With my accumulated capital—maybe now in the low millions if I was lucky—I form an investment group (or partner with smart people) and **buy the Amiga division outright** during liquidation. Not just the patents, but I hire the fired engineers immediately. I keep the Amiga 4000T in development, license the OS properly, and avoid Escom’s mistakes.

**Phase 4: 1995 onward — Run it lean**
I don’t try to compete with Windows PCs head-on (that’s suicide). I position Amiga as the high-end multimedia/workstation and embedded platform it was meant to be—video editing, broadcast graphics, music production (where the Amiga was genuinely dominant in the ’90s). I release a modernized Amiga with a PowerPC or strong 68060 path, keep the OS updated, and build a loyal niche instead of chasing the mass market Commodore couldn’t afford.

**If I’m truly poor and can’t build capital?**
Then I can’t save the company. Period. I’d instead preserve the culture: archive every Amiga disk, join user groups, document the OS source code, and keep the machines running in basements until the open-source era can resurrect them (which, in our real timeline, is kind of what happened with AROS and MorphOS).

So, to answer your question directly: I wouldn’t travel back to be a “hero employee.” I’d travel back to be **the well-funded buyer in 1994 who knows exactly which pieces are worth saving, and has the cash to do it.** The corporation dies either way—the rot was too deep—but the Amiga lives, leaner and smarter, without Irving Gould’s ghost haunting the boardroom.
